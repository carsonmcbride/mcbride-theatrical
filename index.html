About

The person they call when the room has to be right.

Michael McBride has been head lighting designer at the Conference Center in Salt Lake City for twenty-seven years. It seats 21,000, which by seat count makes it the largest theater auditorium in the world, and there's a 900-seat theater attached to it.

He was there before it opened. Nobody had lit the building yet, so there was no rep plot to inherit and nobody to ask. Most designers spend a career in rooms that already work. He got one that didn't exist yet, and then spent twenty-seven years finding out which of those early calls held up.

The building doesn't sit empty between conferences. It runs most weeks of the year: concerts, choir and orchestra programs, broadcasts, devotionals, pageants, and full theatrical productions, often turning over from one to the next in a matter of days. The Christmas concerts are the biggest thing on the calendar. Several nights in a row, tens of thousands of people, then a national PBS broadcast after.

In 2016 he toured Europe with The Tabernacle Choir at Temple Square. Seven cities, five countries, a little over two weeks, including the Berlin Philharmonie and the Musikverein in Vienna. Those halls are a good test of a designer. You arrive with a company of nearly four hundred and a design built for a room ten times the size, and you work with whatever rigging, power, and sightlines the building already has. He has also lit temple open houses and dedications overseas, usually with a few days of access and a crew he's never met.

He takes that experience to other people's projects as well: houses of worship, theatres, touring productions, architects, and integrators.

  • New venues. He's taken a major room from empty building to opening night, then run it for 27 years.
  • Large worship and assembly spaces. Very few designers have lived with one at this scale, daily, for decades.
  • Rooms that run constantly. Rigs built for fast turnover between events that have nothing in common.
  • Camera and eye at once. Broadcast lighting that still works for the people sitting in the room.
  • Touring. One design, a lot of different halls, crews he's meeting that morning.
  • Industry contacts. Manufacturers, rental houses, programmers, and crew around the country.
  • Your side of the table. A straight read on what a vendor is proposing and what it will cost you later.

Services

From the first sketch to the last focus note.

Jobs range from a two-hour second opinion to running the design on a multi-year build. Most of them start with a conversation about what the room needs to do.

01

Production & Concert Design

Theatre, concert, choral, pageant, and large live events. Plot, paperwork, focus, and cueing all the way through opening.

02

Venue & Systems Consulting

New builds and renovations, from someone who has opened one. Fixture and control specs, dimming and network infrastructure, rigging positions, and where things go so people can actually see.

03

Worship, Temple & Ceremonial

Lighting that stays reverent and still lets people see what they came for. Congregational spaces, open houses, and dedications, here and overseas.

04

Broadcast & Camera Lighting

Rooms that hold up under multi-camera coverage and streaming. Color temperature, contrast ratios, and dimming that won't flicker on camera.

05

Touring & Multi-Venue Design

One show, a lot of different rooms. Adapting a design across halls that vary in size, power, and rigging, including tours abroad with local crews.

06

Repertory & Fast Turnover

Rep plots and rigs for venues running back-to-back events, built so tomorrow's show can load in without starting from scratch.

07

Design Review & Owner's Rep

A straight read on someone else's drawings, bids, or budget before you sign anything. Usually the cheapest hour a project spends.

08

Training & Crew Development

Hands-on teaching for staff and volunteers: running the console, rigging safely, keeping up on maintenance, and writing it all down.

How It Works

A straightforward way to work together.

01

A call

No charge. We talk about the room, the schedule, and the budget. Twenty minutes is usually enough to tell whether it's a good fit.

02

Site and scope

A walk through the space, or a look at the drawings if it isn't built yet. Then a written scope with a fixed fee or a day rate.

03

Design

Plot, paperwork, and specs, worked out with your people and your vendors, around the crew and calendar you actually have.

04

In the room

On site through load-in, focus, and cueing. The job isn't done until it's working in front of an audience.
